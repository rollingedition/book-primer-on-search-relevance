# What relevance means

By the end of this chapter you can read a set of relevance judgments critically: say what each grade claims about a product, find where the judgments came from and where they have holes, and decide how far to trust a score computed from them.

[Chapter 1](#search-end-to-end) scored two rankers against {{out:load-the-store:judged pairs}} judgments for [the store](#store-data), which sells furniture and home goods online, and took every grade as fact. On the test queries, [BM25](#rank-with-bm25) averages an [nDCG@10](#score-one-ranking) of {{out:compare-lexical-semantic:bm25 ndcg@10}}. [Semantic retrieval](#search-by-meaning) scores {{out:compare-lexical-semantic:semantic ndcg@10}}. Each of those numbers rests on choices the judgments make without saying so: whose idea of relevance they record, how much a near miss is worth, and what happens to the products nobody judged.

This chapter reads the store's judgments closely. It changes no ranker, so it reads [train and test queries](#store-data) alike: reading judgments isn't tuning, and every fix in this book is still chosen on train queries only. The few tests it runs on the test queries are exploratory, so they describe the judgments and establish no difference ([the book's rule](#when-a-difference-is-established)). [Chapter 3](#compare-runs-in-a-harness) tests the gap between BM25 and semantic retrieval as a planned comparison.

## Judge relevance against the shopper's need {#relevance-is-a-judgment}

A search system returns products that match a query in the way the system defines a match: shared words for lexical retrieval, nearby vectors for semantic retrieval. Information science calls that *system* relevance. It separates that from *topical* relevance, whether a product is about the query's subject, and *situational* relevance, whether it helps with the task at hand ([Saracevic, 2007](https://tefkos.rutgers-sci.domains/Saracevic%20relevance%20pt%20II%20JASIST%20%2707.pdf)). Some argue that system relevance is always perfect, because the system retrieved what the query asked for. Saracevic's answer is "Not so."

A **judgment** is a person's label for one pair of a query and a product, their verdict on it, and a **grade** is the number that stands for the label. The store's judgments come from WANDS, a public product-search dataset, through a sample of its queries and products ([Load the store's data](#store-data)). WANDS's judges label each pair exact, partial or irrelevant, which the book grades 2, 1 and 0. Exact means the product is the kind of thing the query asks for and has every attribute the query names. Partial means it is the right kind of thing but misses an attribute. Irrelevant means it isn't the kind of thing asked for ([Read intent as a product and its modifiers](#intent-in-product-search)). The judge grades the product against the **information need** behind the query's words, the thing the shopper wanted, and not against the words alone. Manning et al. define a document as relevant when it addresses the stated information need, not merely because it contains the query's words ([Manning et al., 2008, chapter 8](https://nlp.stanford.edu/IR-book/html/htmledition/information-retrieval-system-evaluation-1.html)). Evaluation then makes a bargain. It fixes a set of documents, a set of need statements and their judgments, and abstracts away the real users ([Voorhees, 2007](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~brecht/courses/854-Experimental-Performance-Evaluation-2017/readings/trec-cacm-2007.pdf)). The judgments stand in for shoppers here, and every score in this book inherits that bargain.

If matching words were the same as relevance, a product whose text holds every word of the query would be exact. You can test that on every judged pair in the store: for each pair, ask whether the product's name, class, category and description contain every word of the query, with the sample's descriptions cut to about 400 characters.

Try a guess first: of the judged pairs whose text holds every query word, what share did the judges call exact?

Some misses are word forms and spelling, such as `chairs` against `chair` ([chapter 1](#search-end-to-end-in-production)) or the query `ligth bulb`. WANDS records attributes such as color, material and size apart from the text ([Chen et al., 2022, Table 1](https://easychair.org/publications/preprint/wm4R)), and the store keeps a few of them in an `attributes` column, for example `color: clear; material: vinyl` ([data card](../examples/data/wands/README.md)). The text the store's search reads, `lib.store.product_text`, leaves that column out.

For some queries, though, the attributes hold the answer. [Chapter 5](#index-attributes-as-a-field) tests adding attributes to lexical search. The rest of those curtains say `clear` nowhere, in their text or their attributes.

The judges had more to go on. WANDS's annotation guidelines show them each product's name, image and web page ([guidelines](https://github.com/wayfair/WANDS/raw/3b74dcf4ba29ab8ff3e6a50b5b09fc627cb882b5/Product%20Search%20Relevance%20Annotation%20Guidelines.pdf)). The photo or the page may be what told them, but that is our guess, because the data doesn't record which evidence a judge used.

**Working rule:** Treat a judgment as a person's verdict about a need, not a property of the product. Before you blame a ranker for missing an exact product, check whether any field the ranker reads says what the judges saw, and look at the fields it doesn't read, such as attributes. When your judges see more than your ranker does, give the ranker the same evidence where you can, or accept that some exact products will rank low for reasons no change to the ranking can fix.

## Expect judges to disagree on some pairs {#judges-disagree-on-some-pairs}

Because a judgment is a verdict, two people can reach different ones. Each WANDS label is already the majority vote of up to three annotators ([Chen et al., 2022, section 5](https://easychair.org/publications/preprint/wm4R)), and the annotators, who began in moderate agreement, converged with training. Cohen's kappa, a measure of agreement that discounts the agreement expected by chance, rose from 0.467 in their first two months to 0.664 in the third and 0.826 in the fourth ([Table 2](https://easychair.org/publications/preprint/wm4R)). [Chapter 4](#agreement-beyond-chance) computes kappa.

WANDS's file lists some of the store's pairs more than once, each row a majority vote, and for a few the votes differ. The store's sample keeps one row per pair, graded 2 for exact, 1 for partial and 0 for irrelevant, and where the rows differ it keeps the lower grade ([Load the store's data](#store-data)). Here are the pairs whose rows differ, each with the grade the sample kept:

[Example: conflicting-judgments](../examples/conflicting-judgments.py)

Every conflict sits between neighbouring grades, {{out:conflicting-judgments:conflicts between neighbouring grades}}, so the soft spot is the line between two grades, not the gap between exact and irrelevant. The `breakfast bar table` row is one such case: whether a counter height dining table fully matches that query is a fair question about a modifier, not a sign that one vote misread the task.

**Working rule:** Keep one grade per pair and write down the rule that chose it. We recommend the lower grade, as the sample does: it errs against the ranker that ranks the product, not in its favour. When judges disagree across two grades, exact against irrelevant, reread the guidelines with them, because they don't share a definition.

## Read intent as a product and its modifiers {#intent-in-product-search}

Every judgment starts from a reading of what the query asks for. A shopper's **intent** is what they want to do. A widely cited taxonomy of web search sorts intent into navigational (reach a particular site), informational (find information) and transactional (do something on the web), and its author makes no assumption that intent can be inferred from the query with any certainty ([Broder, 2002](https://sigir.org/files/forum/F2002/broder.pdf)). Product search reshapes those kinds. Tsagkias et al. describe navigational product queries as ones that name a serial number or a product, and informational ones as usually mapping to a leaf in the inventory taxonomy or asking about specific attributes. They add that "there is no single taxonomy for eCommerce customer intent", because it depends on the store's business and inventory ([Tsagkias et al., 2020, section 2](https://sigir.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/p04.pdf)).

WANDS writes intent into its labels. A query names a **target entity**, the kind of product, plus **modifiers**, the attributes the shopper asked for. Exact satisfies both. Partial matches only the target entity and fails the modifiers ([Chen et al., 2022, section 3.2](https://easychair.org/publications/preprint/wm4R)). The guidelines' worked example is a query for a kids' chair: a product that is a chair but not for kids is partial ([guidelines](https://github.com/wayfair/WANDS/raw/3b74dcf4ba29ab8ff3e6a50b5b09fc627cb882b5/Product%20Search%20Relevance%20Annotation%20Guidelines.pdf)). Even with that rule, a broad query can be hard to judge. The authors' own example is a shopper who wants an induction cooktop and types `cooktop` ([section 1](https://easychair.org/publications/preprint/wm4R)).

The store's queries cover the range. `ines shag rug w003096809` and `kohler whitehaven farmhouse kitchen sink` name a product line or a model, which makes them navigational. `outdoor light fixtures` names a category. `clear shower curtain` and `48 inch kitchen hood` add a modifier to an entity. The store's queries also carry `query_class`, WANDS's *predicted* class for each query ([Table 1](https://easychair.org/publications/preprint/wm4R)). It is a guess at the entity, says nothing about modifiers, and isn't a judgment. Check how often it agrees with the products the judges called exact:

[Example: predicted-class-check](../examples/predicted-class-check.py)

The predicted class usually agrees with the exact products. For {{out:predicted-class-check:queries where most exact products have the query_class}} of the {{out:predicted-class-check:queries}} queries, most exact products carry it, and for {{out:predicted-class-check:queries where no exact product has the query_class}} queries, none does, and {{out:predicted-class-check:queries without a query_class}} query has no class at all. Read each row the example prints before you count it as an error. `foutains with brick look` is classed as `Wallpaper`, while its exact products are fountains, so the classifier went wrong. `7qt slow cooker` is a naming mismatch instead: the query classes and the product classes use different names for the same thing.

**Working rule:** Read each query as an entity plus modifiers, and check that your ranker gets the entity right before you tune it for modifiers. Use `query_class` as a hint, for a feature or a filter you check against the judgments, and never as a label. When intent is ambiguous, as with `cooktop`, judge against the guidelines' reading and write that reading down.

## Grade relevance instead of yes or no {#graded-relevance}

A binary label says relevant or not. A graded label, such as WANDS's exact, partial or irrelevant, says how relevant. Results differ in how useful they are, so the most relevant ones should be found and ranked first. [Järvelin and Kekäläinen](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xkohout7/Research/clanky_cizi/evaluation/p422-jarvelin.pdf) made the case for grades in 2002. In a store, a near miss is often a fair second choice. A shower curtain that isn't clear, which WANDS grades partial because it is the right kind of product without the attribute asked for ([Read intent as a product and its modifiers](#intent-in-product-search)), is closer to what the shopper wanted than a shower curtain hook. Binary labels force one line through the grades. Count only exact as relevant, and the near miss is worth as little as the hook. Count exact and partial, and it is worth as much as the clear curtain.

The store's query `clear shower curtain` shows both failures. Build three top-10 rankings from its judgments: ten exact products, ten shower curtains judged partial, and ten shower curtain hooks judged irrelevant. Then score each with nDCG@10 ([Score one ranking](#score-one-ranking)) under three labelings: binary with exact as relevant, binary with exact or partial as relevant, and the book's grades, 2 for exact, 1 for partial and 0 for irrelevant, used as gains. A **gain** is what a result at a given position adds to the score before the position discount.

[Example: binary-labels-hide-partial](../examples/binary-labels-hide-partial.py)

Only the grades separate the three rankings. With exact-only labels, the other curtains score the same as the hooks, and with exact-or-partial labels they score the same as the clear curtains. With grades, the clear curtains score {{out:binary-labels-hide-partial:clear curtains, grades}}, the other curtains {{out:binary-labels-hide-partial:other curtains, grades}} and the hooks {{out:binary-labels-hide-partial:hooks, grades}}. When the ideal top 10 is all exact, as it is here with {{out:binary-labels-hide-partial:exact products in the ideal top 10}} exact products in it, a ranking of ten partial products scores the ratio of the partial gain to the exact gain.

**Working rule:** We recommend graded judgments for product search, so a metric can credit a near miss without mistaking it for a match. Collect grades even if you report a binary metric too: you can always collapse grades to yes or no, but not the reverse. Use binary labels when a near miss is worthless to the user, as when the query is a model number and only that model will do.

### Choose the gains on purpose {#gains-are-a-choice}

Grades are labels. Gains are what each label is worth in a metric, and that is a choice. This book uses each grade as its gain, so partial is worth half of exact. WANDS's own paper weights exact, partial and irrelevant as 1.0, 0.5 and 0 ([Chen et al., 2022, Table 3](https://easychair.org/publications/preprint/wm4R)), the same ratio and so the same nDCG. The Shopping Queries Dataset, known as ESCI ([Reddy et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.06588)), has 2.6 million manually labeled query-product judgments over 130 thousand unique queries, and grades differently. It separates a *substitute*, an item that works as a functional substitute, such as fleece for a sweater query, from a *complement*, an item that can be used with an exact one. Its gains are 1.0 for exact, 0.1 for substitute, 0.01 for complement and 0.0 for irrelevant ([Reddy et al., 2022, section 3.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.06588)). WANDS's partial isn't the same as ESCI's substitute, but it is the nearest equivalent.

Score the store's two test runs from chapter 1, [BM25](#rank-with-bm25)'s and semantic retrieval's rankings for every test query, under each of these schemes: the two binary labelings from [Grade relevance instead of yes or no](#graded-relevance), the book's grades, and a partial gain of 0.1 in the spirit of ESCI. The example also runs chapter 3's [permutation test](#permutation-test-on-queries) and [paired t-test](#paired-t-test-on-queries) on semantic retrieval's lead under each scheme, and prints both p-values. Both are [exploratory](#when-a-difference-is-established) like every test here, so they describe each lead and establish none. The semantic run reads the recorded embeddings of the open model `Snowflake/snowflake-arctic-embed-m-v1.5` ([Search by meaning](#search-by-meaning)).

[Example: gain-choice-changes-ndcg](../examples/gain-choice-changes-ndcg.py)

Semantic retrieval leads under every scheme, but the size of its lead depends on the choice. Partial products fill {{out:gain-choice-changes-ndcg:partial share of the bm25 top 10}} of BM25's top-10 places and {{out:gain-choice-changes-ndcg:partial share of the semantic top 10}} of semantic retrieval's, so the partial gain moves both scores a long way. The example doesn't test whether those leads differ, so on {{out:load-the-store:test queries}} test queries, treat the differences between them as possibly noise.

Exact-or-partial labels also saturate, so they separate the rankers on fewer queries. {{out:gain-choice-changes-ndcg:tied queries, exact or partial}} test queries tie under them, against {{out:gain-choice-changes-ndcg:tied queries, grades 2, 1, 0}} under the grades.

**Working rule:** We recommend linear gains, the grades 2, 1 and 0, as a default for product search. Choose a lower partial gain when a near miss is worth little to your shoppers. Whatever you choose, fix it before you compare rankers, report it with every score, and never compare nDCG computed with different gains.

## Read the label mix of each query {#label-mix-per-query}

Gains set what each grade is worth, but not how many grades of each kind a query has. A judgment set isn't one population: each query has its own pool of judged products and its own mix of grades, and the mix decides what a perfect ranking looks like for that query. A broad query, one that names a category many products satisfy, has many exact products. A specific query, one with several modifiers, may have only a handful. An average over queries mixes these different problems, so read the mix before you average anything.

The store's queries aren't a random draw from WANDS. Each was chosen to have 60 to 300 judged products, at least 3 exact and 20 partial judgments, and a predicted class no other chosen query has ([data card](../examples/data/wands/README.md)). Those limits set the floor and the ceiling of the counts that follow. The counts describe the sample, not WANDS as a whole, because queries outside the limits never reach the store. For those, the ideal top 10 includes partial products, so a ranking can score perfectly with partial products in its top 10. For a broad query such as `outdoor light fixtures`, a perfect score needs ten exact products. The same nDCG@10 means a different thing for each.

**Working rule:** Print the label mix per query before you average any metric, and look at both ends. Read a low score on a query with few exact products as a different problem from a low score on a broad query. Keep per-query scores next to every mean; [chapter 3](#read-per-query-differences) shows what averages hide.

## Treat clicks as biased evidence {#clicks-are-not-judgments}

A store logs every click, and clicks cost nothing to collect, so they look like free judgments. They aren't. A click says that a shopper chose to look at a product. It doesn't say the product met the need, and whether a shopper clicks at all depends on where and how the product was shown.

The evidence on how clicks mislead comes from web search. Joachims et al. tracked the eyes and clicks of Cornell undergraduates answering ten set questions with Google, in two phases, with 34 students in the first phase and 22 in the second. In the second phase some saw the top two results swapped, or the whole list reversed ([Joachims et al., 2005](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/people/tj/publications/joachims_etal_05a.pdf)). They concluded that clicks are "informative but biased."

The first bias is **position bias**: people click higher-ranked results more, partly because they trust the ranking. Joachims et al. call the effect a "trust bias," which draws clicks to highly ranked links even when other abstracts the user viewed are more relevant. The authors also found that relative preferences, such as a clicked result over a skipped one above it, are reasonably accurate on average.

In a large experiment that reordered results on a web search engine, Craswell et al. found that a **cascade model** explained position bias best in the top ranks. In that model, users read results from top to bottom and leave at the first worthwhile document ([Craswell et al., 2008](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=156688)).

**Presentation bias** goes beyond position. On a portion of Google's web search traffic, results with more attractive titles, meaning more of the query's words in bold, drew more clicks, even after controlling for position and for relevance as people judged it ([Yue et al., 2010](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www2010/proceedings/www/p1011.pdf)). In a store, images and prices are the nearest analogue to titles. That is our inference, not a measured result.

WANDS has no click data, so the store can't measure these biases. You can still see what a click log would record. Take the store's judgments for `clear shower curtain`, and apply the cascade model in its strictest form: a shopper reads from the top, clicks the first exact product and leaves. This is a thought experiment with one fixed rule, not a model of real shoppers. [Chapter 11](#simulate-interleaving-with-clicks) simulates clicks with probabilities. Run the thought experiment on BM25's ranking and on semantic retrieval's, and read the two click logs side by side:

[Example: cascade-clicks](../examples/cascade-clicks.py)

A click log records what the ranker showed first as much as what the shopper wanted. Under [BM25](#rank-with-bm25)'s ranking, the shopper clicks rank {{out:cascade-clicks:bm25 clicked rank}}, `{{out:cascade-clicks:bm25 clicked product}}`, and never reaches the {{out:cascade-clicks:bm25 exact products shown below the click}} exact products below it. In the click log, those exact products look the same as BM25's ranks 8 to 10, shower curtain hooks judged irrelevant ([Rank products with BM25](#rank-with-bm25)), which nobody clicked either.

Under [semantic retrieval](#search-by-meaning)'s ranking, the click lands at rank {{out:cascade-clicks:semantic clicked rank}}, on `{{out:cascade-clicks:semantic clicked product}}`. Two rankings from the same catalog produce two click logs that credit different products.

WANDS's authors did not build their dataset from logs, for the position bias described above ([Check the results against the judgments](#check-against-judgments)). Clicks still carry information, though. In WANDS, 62.47% of the candidates taken from click logs were judged exact, against 30.95% of those taken from open-source ranking systems ([Chen et al., 2022, Table 4](https://easychair.org/publications/preprint/wm4R)).

Correcting clicks for bias is a research field of its own, unbiased learning to rank. On a large search dataset released by Baidu, those methods improved click prediction but did not consistently improve ranking performance as experts judged it ([Hager et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.02543)).

**Working rule:** Use judgments, not clicks, as the reference for offline evaluation. Use clicks as relative evidence, a clicked product over a skipped one ranked above it, and never read a missing click as a sign that a product is irrelevant. If you train on clicks, check the result against judgments.

## Know where the judgments came from {#pooled-judgments}

Nobody judges every product for every query, so test collections judge a **pool** instead. In TREC, the Text REtrieval Conference's shared evaluations, a topic's pool is the union of the top-ranked documents each participant retrieved, usually the top 100, and every document outside the pool counts as not relevant. Voorhees argues that systems can still be compared fairly when every unjudged document counts as not relevant, because pools hold enough of the relevant documents. The same article notes that pooling can't produce reusable test collections for arbitrarily large document sets ([Voorhees, 2007](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~brecht/courses/854-Experimental-Performance-Evaluation-2017/readings/trec-cacm-2007.pdf)). Zobel tested the fairness claim. With pooled judgments, the relative performance of systems looks reliable, but recall is overestimated, since many relevant documents have probably never been found ([Zobel, 1998](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/courses/cs574/2002fa/assignments.org/zobel-sigir98.pdf)).

WANDS built its pool in two steps ([Chen et al., 2022, sections 4.2 and 4.3](https://easychair.org/publications/preprint/wm4R)). The first took the products shoppers had clicked or added to their carts, plus the results of an open-source lexical search engine, Solr, and of a neural retrieval system. The second mined the whole catalog again with the same two systems, and added every product in the class that a query classification model predicted for the query. That second step produced most of WANDS's labels, and mostly near misses: 4.12% of its labels were exact and 63.99% partial ([Table 5](https://easychair.org/publications/preprint/wm4R)). Partial is the commonest label in the store's sample too ([Read the label mix of each query](#label-mix-per-query)). The authors argue that completeness matters for product search, because "incompleteness in Product Search dataset does negatively impact the discriminative power of the evaluation" ([section 3.1](https://easychair.org/publications/preprint/wm4R)).

A pool belongs to a query. A product without a judgment for a query is **unjudged** for it, and a product judged for one query can be unjudged for a similar one. Two of the store's queries show this: the train query `trundle daybed` and the test query `full bed with trundle`. Count the products judged exact for the first that have no judgment for the second, and see how the judges graded the ones that do:

[Example: trundle-daybeds-unjudged](../examples/trundle-daybeds-unjudged.py)

A metric that scores an unjudged product as 0 is guessing about it, and here the guess matters. By our reading of the guidelines' rule, a daybed with a trundle is often the right kind of bed without the size the query asks for. Of the {{out:trundle-daybeds-unjudged:exact for trundle daybed}} products judged exact for `trundle daybed`, {{out:trundle-daybeds-unjudged:of them unjudged for full bed with trundle}} have no judgment for `full bed with trundle`, among them the two with the lowest product numbers, `{{out:trundle-daybeds-unjudged:first unjudged daybed}}` and `{{out:trundle-daybeds-unjudged:second unjudged daybed}}`. Of the {{out:trundle-daybeds-unjudged:of them judged for full bed with trundle}} that do, {{out:trundle-daybeds-unjudged:judged partial}} are partial. If the unjudged daybeds resemble the judged ones, most of them would be partial too, yet a metric gives each one grade 0.

Zobel warns that pooled judgments can underestimate systems that didn't contribute to the pool ([Zobel, 1998](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/courses/cs574/2002fa/assignments.org/zobel-sigir98.pdf)). That makes judgments fairest to rankers like the systems that built the pool: for WANDS, shoppers' clicks, a lexical engine, a neural retriever and a query classifier. It is a tendency, not a law. Here BM25, the ranker most like WANDS's lexical engine, has the lower judged@10 of the two rankers ([Count unjudged products](#unjudged-results)). Our guess is that the store's BM25 reads different fields than the pool's engine did, so it ranks different products high.

**Working rule:** Find out where your pool came from before you trust a comparison. When you evaluate a ranker unlike the systems that built it, expect it to surface products nobody judged, and plan to judge them.

## Count unjudged products before you compare scores {#unjudged-results}

A product nobody judged for a query is [unjudged](#pooled-judgments): its grade is unknown, not 0. Standard metrics can't tell the difference. They score an unjudged product exactly as they score one judged irrelevant, and Buckley and Voorhees warn that such metrics become unreliable when judgments are very incomplete ([Buckley and Voorhees, 2004](https://tsapps.nist.gov/publication/get_pdf.cfm?pub_id=150469)). Two diagnostics are standard in evaluation tools ([ir-measures](https://ir-measur.es/en/latest/measures.html)). **Judged@k** is the share of the top k that has a judgment of any grade:

```text
judged@k = (number of the top k products that have a judgment for the query) / k
```

The second diagnostic drops the unjudged products before scoring, so the judged ones move up. ir-measures offers it as the `judged_only` option, and we call the result a **condensed** list. Buckley and Voorhees's bpref measure works in the same spirit: it uses judged documents only, and they found it more robust to incomplete judgments.

The mean for [semantic retrieval](#search-by-meaning) is {{out:compare-lexical-semantic:semantic judged@10}}. One property of the store's data keeps those numbers high. Its catalog is almost all judged: {{out:load-the-store:products judged for at least one query}} of {{out:load-the-store:products}} products have a judgment for at least one query ([Load the store's data](#store-data)). Expect a full catalog to put more unjudged products in the top 10s.

## What goes wrong in production {#what-relevance-means-in-production}

**Judgments go stale.** A store's catalog and its shoppers keep changing, and relevance depends on more than the query. Tsagkias et al. name the user, time, query and context, and give an example: a query for jackets has different relevant products in winter than in summer ([Tsagkias et al., 2020, section 3.1.1](https://sigir.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/p04.pdf)). Products enter the catalog unjudged, and products that leave it stay in the judgment set. Track [judged@10](#unjudged-results) for your production ranker over time, and judge again when it falls.

**Click labels confirm the ranking you have.** A ranker trained on clicks logged under the current ranking learns that ranking's order along with the shoppers' preferences. Keep human judgments as the reference that clicks are checked against ([Treat clicks as biased evidence](#clicks-are-not-judgments)).

**Guidelines shape the judgments.** WANDS's annotators needed four months to reach near-perfect agreement ([Chen et al., 2022, Table 2](https://easychair.org/publications/preprint/wm4R)). Small choices in the guidelines shape the whole set. WANDS told annotators to ignore misspellings and judge the example as written ([guidelines](https://github.com/wayfair/WANDS/raw/3b74dcf4ba29ab8ff3e6a50b5b09fc627cb882b5/Product%20Search%20Relevance%20Annotation%20Guidelines.pdf)), so its judgments assume a reader who understands `ligth bulb`. A ranker that can't will score low on such queries, which is fair to the shopper. [Chapter 8](#correct-spelling-from-catalog) builds a spelling corrector and shows what it still gets wrong.

**Your ranker sees less than your judges.** The judges saw images and product pages, while the store's rankers read text without the attributes ([chapter 5 tests adding them](#index-attributes-as-a-field)). Any large language model (LLM) asked to judge from the same text sees what the ranker sees. Some disagreement between such a system and the human judgments comes from that gap, not from the system. Check which fields hold the attributes your judges rewarded before you blame the ranker.

**Filling holes with a model still needs human judgments.** Gienapp et al. fine-tune a small classifier for each query on that query's human judgments, and use it to judge the unjudged products. On TREC Robust04 and the TREC Deep Learning collections of 2019 and 2020, they report that as few as 128 initial human judgments per topic improve the comparability of models, and that the approach is more reliable than prompted LLM judges ([Gienapp et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.04633)). Soboroff's advice on LLMs is blunter: "don't use LLMs to create relevance judgments for TREC-style evaluations" ([Soboroff, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.15133)). [Chapter 4](#read-llm-error-direction) measures LLM judgments against the store's human ones before trusting them.

**Working rule:** Date your judgments, track judged@10 in production, and budget for judging again.

## Decisions this chapter made {#what-relevance-means-decisions}

Each choice below has a reason, and each is a default that later chapters keep unless a measurement on train queries says otherwise.

- **Human judgments are the reference.** Every headline score in this book is computed on them, including scores for rankers built with LLMs. Clicks are evidence about shoppers, biased by position and presentation, and never serve as labels.
- **Grades 2, 1 and 0 are the gains,** so partial is worth half of exact, the same ratio as WANDS's own 1.0, 0.5 and 0. Binary metrics count exact only, so **relevant** means exact unless a sentence says otherwise.
- **One grade per pair.** When WANDS lists a pair more than once with different labels, the sample keeps the lower grade, which errs against the ranker.
- **`query_class` is a hint,** never a label and never a measure of intent. Some of its values are wrong.
- **Unjudged counts as grade 0, with judged@10 beside every nDCG@10.** Condensed scores are a diagnostic only. The cure for unjudged products in a top 10 is to have them judged.
- **Rankers read the product text, not the attributes.** [Chapter 5](#index-attributes-as-a-field) tests adding them as a field and keeps them out of the BM25 text. The judges also saw images and product pages, so some exact products give a text-only ranker little to match.
- **Read any judgment, tune on train.** Reading test judgments changes no ranker; choosing a fix from them would.
